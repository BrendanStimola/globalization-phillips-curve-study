\section{Abstract}

This paper studies whether globalization and China joining the WTO in particular weakens the Phillips Curve relationship globally and in the United States, and how the Phillips Curve evolved over time. This was achieved by using different measures for inflation, different variables and different countries. The first model includes unemployment, trade to GDP, interaction effects, and lagged inflation for the US from 1990 to 2026. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression is used to estimate the inflation dynamics. The second model uses China's accession into the WTO for a structural break and a two stage least squares (2SLS) to fix possible endogeneity in the model. The model uses 2 variables from 14 countries: unemployment and trade to gross domestic product (GDP). To measure a structural break with the second model there was a Wald test to check for a structural break in the variables after 2001. The Instrumental Variables (IV) used were: labor force participation rate (LFPR), lagged unemployment, trade to GDP rate and inflation rate. Finally the tariff rate was used as a variable to paint a picture of how strong global trade was. The final model replaces inflation with the inflation gap ($\pi-\overline{\pi}$) and uses the same 14 countries from model 2, model 2 also uses the same independent variables as model 1. The results suggest that inflation is highly persistent and that globalization weakens the relationship between unemployment and inflation. The Phillips Curve remains present Globally/US, but globalization weakens the relationship between unemployment and inflation. These findings are consistent with theories that the Phillips Curve has structurally changed after China joined the WTO due to a more globalized economy. 

\section{Introduction}

The Phillips Curve is the theory that unemployment and inflation are negatively correlated\autocite{investopedia_phillips}. Modern-day economists care because this relationship has ripple effects everywhere, from Federal Reserve (Fed) policy to inflation control and employment. The present issue is that the relationship may have weakened due to a variety of reasons\autocite{BOC}. Most of them have to do with how globalization affects prices in an economy. Across the last years economists have noticed how the curve has gotten flatter\autocite{Cleveland-fed}. Though recently, some have noted that the Phillips Curve got steeper after COVID\autocite{Chicago-fed} The research question is does increasing trade to GDP, specifically after China joined the World Trade Organization (WTO)\autocite{cfr2021} change how powerful the Phillips Curve is? The baseline model utilizes an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) framework to isolate the relationship between trade to GDP and the unemployment rate. The second model uses China joining the WTO, which is a pivotal moment in globalization to see the effect on inflation and unemployment globally. The third model does the same thing as the second except it replaces inflation with the gap between inflation and average inflation and adds more variables. We hypothesized that the Phillips Curve would have weakened over time due to globalization. Overall, the question we have in our research paper is, does globalization and China's rise into the WTO effect Phillips Curves in just the US and globally? 

\section{Literature Review}

In the modern day studies about the Phillips Curve and how it has been affected by globalization can be split into two different sides. One side argues that the Phillips Curve has weakened due to globalization and offshoring\autocite{substack_offshoring}. The other side argues that the shift in the Phillips Curve is not due to globalization but shifts in the countries own economy.  Most modern day studies say that globalization is flattening the Phillips Curve\autocite{gaiotti2010}. The IJCB as it is also known attributes this to decoupling demand and supply from domestic markets. Some also state how the output gaps effect on inflation is shrinking because of globalization. Causing pay and unemployment to be based less on the domestic economy\autocite{Kohlscheen2022}. The "Becker Friedman Institute" studies how regional Phillips Curves hold up, and came to the conclusion that 40 percent of the decline in correlation is due to imports\autocite{uchicago_dynamics}. According to science direct, when markets shift production, low-cost manufacturing gets hit the hardest by cost saving actions. According to a paper published by the International Journal of Central Banking\autocite{gaiotti2010}, in Italian manufacturing firms. The increase in globalization did most likely not cause a flattening in the Phillips Curve. On the other hand the Cleveland Fed \autocite{Cleveland-fed} attributes the flattening of the curve to monetary policy and how that affects the household welfare, along with reasons not attributed to monetary policy. Researchers at the Chicago fed\autocite{Chicago-fed} argue that the Phillips Curve has gotten steeper recently, especially after COVID. They attribute this steepening to a rise in the unemployment gap, which is the difference between the country's current unemployment and the long-term trend identified by the OECD November 2019 economic outlook.

\section{Economic Background}

Gross domestic product, also known as GDP is the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a specific time period\autocite{FRED_GDP}. Inflation is the year over year change in the consumer price index. The consumer price index (CPI) uses a basket of goods and services to see the level of the price in an economy\autocite{FRED_CPI, bls_cpi}. We measured the year over year change in the CPI then used each countries inflation for checking for the Phillips Curve. Unemployment is the percent of the labor force that is looking for work but can not find it\autocite{FRED_UNRATE, econosphere_lfpr}. This percent was used and compared with inflation to understand the Phillips Curve relationship. Trade to GDP is measured as:
$$(\frac{Imports+Exports}{GDP})*100$$
This measures the amount of economic activity that is due to trade\autocite{FRED_EXPGS, FRED_IMPGS}. This is used as a proxy for globalization as it measures the percent of the economy that is reliant on trade\autocite{gravel_trade}. Lagged inflation is inflation shifted one period. This is used to see how much of today's inflation is depended on the inflation of the past. This is important because it shows how inflation expectations affect inflation in the long-term. The LFPR is measured as:
$$(\frac{employed+unemployed}{working-age-population})*100$$
This measures the percent of an economy that tries or does get work. The tariff rate, is the average tariff charged for an item going into a country. The equation of the Phillips Curve uses multiple variables mentioned above (see below). The equation for the Phillips Curve is:
$$
\pi_t=\pi^e_t-\alpha(u_t-u_n)+v_t
$$
$\pi^e_t$ are the current expectations of inflation. $\alpha$ is the sensitivity of the Phillips Curve, $u_t$ and $u_n$ are the current unemployment and the natural rate of unemployment respectively; And $v_t$ are shocks to the economy (oil shocks etc..). China joining the WTO was a very important moment in history as it marked the beginning of an influx of low-cost imports from China due to increased supply and lower domestic demand to the USA.

\section{Methodology}

For the regression method OLS was chosen because after using HAC standard errors, OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE)\autocite{builtin_ols}. The purpose was to analyze the relationship between inflation, the unemployment rate and how globalized an economy is compared to the size of its economy. The interaction term tests to what degree globalization affects the Phillips Curve relationship. Two-way fixed effects (2WFE) was also used to account for different conditions over time and different places. The equation for the first model is:
\begin{align*}
\Delta\pi_t =
& \ \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Unemployment}_t + \beta_2 \text{Trade}_t \\
& + \beta_3 \text{Interaction}_t + \beta_4 \Delta\text{Inflation}_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t
\end{align*}
The equation for the second model is:
\begin{align*}
\pi_t =
& \ \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Unemployment}_t + \beta_2 \text{Trade} + \varepsilon_t
\end{align*}
The equation for the third model is:
\begin{align*}
\pi_t-\overline{\pi}=
& \ \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Unemployment}_t + \beta_2 \text{Trade}_t \\
& + \beta_3 \text{Interaction}_t + \beta_4 \text{Inflation}_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t
\end{align*}
Where $\pi_t$ is the current inflation. This equation uses the betas as coefficients to measure the line of best fit. Each variable is multiplied by its beta and then the error is added to try to estimate the inflation or inflation gap ($\pi_t-\overline{\pi}$). The average inflation in the inflation gap is the average of inflation in the countries from 1954-2026.  The average inflation is calculated by taking the average inflation from each country
\newline
\\
The data used was collected from the World Bank database for most of the data, and for the first model federal reserve economic data (FRED) was used to look at the US data points. 
\newline
\\\
The local US-only panel used quarterly data from 1990 to 2026. There were a total of 144 observations in this model. This model used a base OLS to look at how the Phillips curve behaved between 1990 and 2026.  Along with the base OLS, a Wald test was used on the local model to check for a structural break after 2001\autocite{statshowto_wald}.
\newline
\\
The global panel used 14 countries for models 2 and 3: U.S., Canada, UK, Germany, France, Spain, Italy, the Netherlands, Japan, South Korea, Australia, Mexico, Vietnam and Singapore. The countries were observed from 1990-2026 annually. Some data had to be dropped due to the lack of economic data in some countries. In total there were 345 observations in models 2 and 3.
\newline
\\
The global panel used 2SLS and HAC standard errors to account for endogeneity, autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in the model. HAC standard errors stand for Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent. The standard errors try to account for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity in the model. 2SLS works by recalculating the variables from other variables to get rid of endogeneity\autocite{timeseries_2sls}. 
\newline
\\
2WFE were used in models 2 and 3 to get rid of the differences due to time and places in the data\autocite{metricgate_2wfe}. For example 2WFE would compare data in the same time period or country to account for the difference in data. Recessions happen which could affect the data over time, and in different countries the economy is run differently so that has adjusted for.

\subsection{The normal Phillips Curve (with trade to GDP and interaction term) from 1954 to 2026}
In model 1, we are just observing the base results from the US, though it is important to note that HAC standard errors were used to correct for the high Durbin-Watson. In this model the unemployment is negative and statistically significant, meaning that the Phillips Curve does still exist but maybe just weaker. The lagged inflation is very close to 0.5 and has a p-value less than 0.05 meaning inflation is very dependent on inflation from the past. The trade term is barely negative but not statistically significant, the p-value was 0.923, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis that trade to GDP has an effect on inflation. The trade times unemployment rate though yielded economically meaningful results. The interaction term is positive meaning globalization does flatten the Phillips Curve, and the p-value is less than 0.01 meaning it is statistically significant. While it is important to note that the US only Wald Test came back negative meaning that in the US there was possible no structural break. This could be due to the fact that the Wald test was only run from 1990-2026. It also should be noted that before first differencing to fix the lagged inflation, the variable was above 1 meaning it was a non-stationary variable.
\subsection{The before and after China joined WTO in 14 countries 2SLS Model}
In model 2 there were economicly viable results. The global Wald test came back positive. Along with results from the 2WFE and 2SLS. The 2SLS model suggests that especially in post-2001 there were significant and important findings, while some of the variables were not significant. The F-statistic and more data suggests that there was a structural break after 2001, along with this not being based on endogeneity. To be specific the instrumental variables were significant (meaning they could predict unemployment and trade to GDP), yet they could not use that data to predict inflation. Suggesting a more complex and nuanced interpretation. This means that globally unemployment and trade to GDP were not as good predictors of inflation compared to what was previously thought.
\subsection{Inflation gap model in 14 countries}

The reason for this is once the inflation gap is accounted for all that is left is the short-term trends that makes unemployment harder to track. Once the long-term movement is removed the pattern is very cyclical and could cause the model to not function and give results with weaker explanatory power. the only significant variable is lagged inflation, proving that lagged inflation affects current inflation through cyclical patterns.

\section{Limitations}
Unfortunately there are also limitations. There are missing variables such as expectations for unemployment and inflation. Monetary and fiscal policy could also alter the Phillips Curve. There could be omitted variable bias (OVB), OVB is when there are variables that are not in a model but affect the models. Endogeneity is when two variables effect each other and it is impossible to tell what variable affects the other. Endogeneity is present in this paper because of the core relationship between inflation and unemployment that is getting studied. The economy also structurally changes over time which could affect the Phillips Curve. This is also only a simplified model of the economy, this model doesn't take in enough factors to better represent the economy. The trade to GDP doesn't take into account re-exports, re-exports could artificially boosting the trade to GDP number. Using a dummy variable could also hide certain long-term trend affecting the data. Using the dates from 2001-2026 could be skewed due to the 3 recessions and the inflation spike in the aftermath of COVID-19. Also in the third model, the addition of lagged inflation proved that the break was not on the cyclical level, meaning that is it possible that no break occurred.

\section{Discussion}

Looking at the data it is a possible to see a Phillips Curve that has revived in certain periods, but is still active despite the theories about globalization. The models also reveals how China joining the WTO and possibly the dot-com crash, lowered baseline inflation over the next 20 years, but didn't affect the Phillips Curve. Though, by adding lagged inflation is can be observed that the important variables become insignificant. Likely meaning that there was a break just not at the cyclical level. Lagged inflation is also very persistent and means that expectations and consumer decisions are very important for central bank policy and inflation control. When putting the inflation gap in the equation the p-values become weaker, and the coefficients become flatter. This is backed up by the Wald test, showing that after 2001 the relationships in the global economy fundamentally shifted.

\section{Conclusion}

In the global economy, The accession of China into the WTO, suggests a structural break in the Phillips Curve relationship. The Phillips Curve changes throughout different time "regimes" using different ways to measure inflation. The findings were, China joining the WTO, caused a global decouple in the Phillips curve. The Phillips Curve is still around but has been flattened and strengthened throughout different time periods and different places, likely due to the fact that the Phillips Curve was not cyclical. This could be interpreted as firms offshoring employment to low-cost areas raising unemployment\autocite{sciencedirect_hetero}. Firms would also be using cheaper goods lowering inflation. In the two models not using China's rise, inflation was heavily dependent on lagged inflation. The broader macro meaning is when firms can offshore hiring for cheaper costs, inflation declines as international supply rises and domestic stayed steady, because the glut of goods was so great domestic prices were lowered and domestic unemployment in the non-cyclical sector was raised due to off-shored supply. This research could be continued by looking at the Phillips Curve in different time periods and different countries.
